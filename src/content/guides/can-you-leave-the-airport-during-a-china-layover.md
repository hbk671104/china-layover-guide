## The short answer

If you're transiting through mainland China to a third country or region, and your passport is from one of the **55 eligible countries**, you can **leave the airport without a visa for up to 240 hours (10 days)**: no stay inside the terminal required. For most layover travelers, the real question isn't *"am I allowed?"* but *"do I actually have enough hours to make it worthwhile?"*

## Do you need a visa to walk out the door?

**No, in most cases.** China's 240-hour visa-free transit policy lets eligible travelers enter at any of **65 designated ports** across **24 provinces** and travel in the permitted areas, visa-free. You don't need a visa, and you don't need to stay airside.

You need four things:

1. **An eligible passport**: one of 55 countries, including the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, and most of Europe.
2. **A confirmed onward ticket to a third country or region**, departing within 240 hours (Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan count as separate regions).
3. **Entry and exit through designated ports.**
4. **A passport valid for at least 3 months** (some airlines require 6: carry a buffer).

> Not on the transit list? Many nationalities qualify for China's separate **30-day visa-free entry** instead, which is simpler and has no third-country rule. See [30-day visa-free entry vs 240-hour transit](/guides/30-day-visa-free-vs-240-hour-transit/).

## The golden rule for a city-visit layover

This is the math that decides everything:

**A useful city visit needs 8+ hours at the airport.** Here's why: every hour is accounted for:

| What you'll spend time on | Typical time |
|---|---|
| Land, disembark, clear immigration | 45–90 minutes |
| Store luggage / exit the terminal | 15–30 minutes |
| Transfer into the city | 35–60 minutes each way |
| Actually exploring the city | **the point** |
| Transfer back | 35–60 minutes |
| Return buffer (check-in, security, exit immigration) | 2–3 hours |

**Under 6 hours: stay airside.** You'd reach the city and turn straight around: a stressed commute, not a visit.

**6–7 hours: possible but tight.** One attraction, done properly. You need discipline and a clear return plan.

**8–12 hours: the sweet spot.** A real taste of one city: the Bund in Shanghai, a hutong walk and duck lunch in Beijing, dim sum in Guangzhou.

**12+ hours: a genuine city day.** Multiple sights, a proper meal, even a Great Wall run from Beijing.

> **Cross-airport transfers eat everything.** If your inbound and outbound are at different airports (Shanghai PVG vs SHA, or Beijing PEK vs PKX) treat that as a commute across the city, and don't add sightseeing unless you have 12+ hours.

## The step-by-step way out

1. **At check-in**, mention your plan to use visa-free transit. The airline will check your onward ticket before boarding, because they're fined if you get denied entry.
2. **On board**, fill in the **Arrival Card**.
3. **At immigration**, join the **visa-free transit** lane. Show your passport, arrival card, and onward ticket. The officer stamps a temporary entry permit (usually 5–10 minutes if your papers are in order).
4. **Store your bags**: most major airports have left-luggage counters (around ¥15–30 per bag).
5. **Go**, metro or Airport Express into the city, one destination, then straight back.

## Does your nationality actually qualify?

Check the two lists. They overlap but aren't identical:

- **55-country transit list**, includes **US citizens** (notably).
- **30-day visa-free list**, around 50 countries, including most of the EU, the UK, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, and South Korea.

If you're on the 30-day list, use that instead. It's simpler and lets you return to your origin country, unlike the transit rule. For the full comparison, see [30-day visa-free entry vs 240-hour transit](/guides/30-day-visa-free-vs-240-hour-transit/).

## The three mistakes that ruin a city-visit layover

1. **Forgetting the third-country rule.** London → Shanghai → London doesn't qualify. London → Shanghai → Tokyo does. This is the #1 reason travelers get denied.
2. **Underestimating immigration queues.** The transit lane can take 60–90 minutes at peak. Plan as if it will.
3. **Ignoring the return buffer.** Be back at the airport **2.5–3 hours before an international departure**, exit immigration plus security re-clearance regularly stacks 45+ minutes in the evening wave.

## Bottom line

Yes, for most eligible travelers on a China layover, you can absolutely leave the airport. The question to ask yourself is whether you have **8+ usable hours** and a **third-country onward ticket**. If so, don't waste the stopover: pick a city route and go.
